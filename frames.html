The adoption flight

AccessBetter work

Adoption (people taking a tool into the work they do every week) is a flight. A race is the wrong picture: a race has winners, and most people lose. Everyone already has a ticket, and the ticket is access: the tools are already there. The destination is the same for everyone: better work. The job is getting people on board from wherever they stand, and each stage has its own lever.

FRAME-01 · Three stages, nine checkpoints

Each stage has three checkpoints, so you can see where inside a stage someone sits. The checkpoints mark progress on the same flight; none of them ranks one person above another. AI here means any AI a person is allowed to use for their work, the company's own tool or an approved one.

  2. On the ground · Not flying yet

    They have not started, or do not see the point.

    The lever: a plain-words reason to care, tied to their own work. Show the twenty minutes it saves on a report they already write, not what the tool can do in general.

    • No ticketHow you tellHas no AI they are allowed to use for work yet.The leverAccess, and a reason to use AI in the first week.
    • Ticket heldHow you tellIs allowed to use AI for work, but has not tried it yet.The leverA first task done side by side with someone who already uses AI.
    • Checked inHow you tellTried AI once or twice, then stopped.The leverA second task that fits their actual week.

  3. At the gate · Ticket in hand

    They have access, but do not know which tool or which tested prompt to reach for. A tested prompt is a written request that has been checked against cases before anyone relies on it.

    The lever: a first win, and a library they can search. The library is the one shared place where tested prompts are kept.

    • Through securityHow you tellIs still working out what AI is allowed for, or has just read the rules.The leverA worked example from their own role.
    • Waiting to boardHow you tellUses AI only in the weeks a reminder goes out.The leverOne recurring task tied to a day of the week.
    • BoardingHow you tellHas used AI once on real work that went to someone else.The leverMake that recurring task part of their routine.

  4. In the air · Using it well every week

    They reach for it without being reminded, on work that matters to them.

    The lever: they become the advocates, the colleagues others ask first, and their work seeds the next tested prompts.

    • TakeoffHow you tellUses AI every week on one task, for a month or more.The leverA second task, and a place to share what works.
    • Above the cloudsHow you tellUses AI on several tasks and shares what works.The leverAsk them to show their team.
    • Frequent flyerHow you tellAI is part of how they work every week, and colleagues come to them with questions.The leverGive them a prompt to look after in the library, and point new starters their way.

Check-in

Five questions about your stage, then one about your checkpoint, one at a time, and you get your place on the flight with your next step.

Your answers stay in this browser: nothing is sent or stored.

  6. Which sounds most like you?

    Not flying yet

    • I am allowed to use AI, but have not tried it yet
    • I tried it once or twice, then stopped

    At the gate

    • I am still working out what AI is allowed for
    • I mostly use AI when a reminder or a task pushes me

    In the air

    • I use AI on several tasks and share what works

How the answers map to stages. "No, or not yet" to the first question: Not flying yet. Otherwise each first option counts toward In the air, each second toward At the gate, each third toward Not flying yet, and the most common wins. A tie goes to the earlier stage. The last question then places you at a checkpoint in that stage.

Counting who is where. A check-in, where people say for themselves where they stand, gives a fast picture of the shape. The sources give the true one: the usage dashboards show who has access and separate Ticket held, Checked in, Waiting to board and Takeoff; the library's records show Boarding, Above the clouds and Frequent flyer; the question log shows Through security. Where the check-in and the sources disagree, the gap is itself a finding.

What the shape tells you.

  • Most at No ticket or Ticket held: the problem is access and the first week; training does nothing yet.

  • Most waiting to board: the problem is habit. Reminders got them here and will not take them further; the lever is one recurring task per person, tied to a weekday.

  • Most at Boarding or Takeoff, and nobody a Frequent flyer: the problem is sharing; the lever is the library and showing work.

  • A few Frequent flyers and many waiting to board: pair them.

  • Counted again a month later, the people who moved up a checkpoint are the rollout measure.

Before the words, the structure

A song is recognised by its shape long before the words arrive. A prompt that works is written the same way: the structure is fixed first, and the person using it brings the words. A prompt is the request as written to an AI assistant.

FRAME-02 · The song bar, and each part's share of the writing grade
Key10 Intro20 Verse15 Chorus25 Outro10
Rhythm, the whole length20

  1. Key and instruments · Set before it all

    The material and the tools it works with: the files, notes or examples the person hands over, and what the prompt is allowed to use.

    10 of the 100 points on the writing grade.

  2. Intro · Role and task

    Who it is, who it writes for, and what it is for.

    20 of the 100 points on the writing grade.

  3. Verse · The process

    The steps, numbered, in the order they are done.

    15 of the 100 points on the writing grade.

  4. Chorus · The output

    Form, length and shape, with one worked example, so the answer comes back the same way every time.

    25 of the 100 points on the writing grade: the largest share.

  5. Outro · The stop condition

    What it declines, when it asks instead of guessing, and where it hands back to a person.

    10 of the 100 points on the writing grade.

  6. Rhythm · Running the whole length

    The rules and the style, kept from the first line to the last.

    20 of the 100 points on the writing grade.

The cast.

  • The composer writes the structure and is rarely heard on the recording.
  • The performer uses it, and the credit stays with them.
  • The words are the performer's own content.
  • The sheet is one tested structure anyone can pick up and play.
  • The repertoire is the library, where good songs become the foundation of the next.

Two ways a song gets written. The composer starts with the structure and the performer adds the words, or the performer arrives with a half-written prompt and the composer builds the structure around it. Both roads end at the same song.

The rehearsal

Nobody can hear a prompt's output, so every sheet is rehearsed on five cases written first: three ordinary, one edge case (unusual but fair), and one it should decline.

  • Ordinary
  • Ordinary

It passes when four of five match and it declines the one it should.

Keeping score

FRAME-02 · The loop
01Write 02Grade the sheet 03Rehearse 04Grade the performance 05Verdict 06Reuse

Where it breaks in use, the next version starts back at Write.

  1. 01 · Write

    Write the sheet: the structure first, in the order of the song bar.

  2. 02 · Grade the sheet

    Score the writing out of 100 against the song bar. Below 60, it goes back to be rewritten before any test.

  3. 03 · Rehearse

    Run it on the five cases written first: three ordinary, one edge, one it should decline.

  4. 04 · Grade the performance

    Score the output out of 100: quality 60, speed 20, cost 20, on the smallest model that passed.

  5. 05 · Verdict

    • Ready enters the repertoire.
    • Refine gets a small fix and is rehearsed again.
    • Rewrite means the structure is wrong, so it goes back to Write.
    • Retire means it is no longer needed, or a better sheet covers it.

  6. 06 · Reuse

    Anyone can pick the sheet up and play it. Where it breaks in use, the break starts the next version.

The brief before the prompt

Most weak prompts are weak briefs. A brief is the short written account of what is wanted, set down before any prompt. Before touching any tool, answer four questions in twenty seconds.

20
Who reads this?
What must it never contain?
What shape does it come back in?
Where does it stop?

The gate rule in two lines. The expected answer is agreed with the person asking before the first run, and where no standard exists, their own "what good looks like" becomes the standard. Time-boxed work (work with a deadline that will not wait for the full test) ships through the draft gate; only the library gate makes it reusable.

  1. Ask · 01 · Request received

    Taken down in the words it arrived in, before anyone decides what it means.

  2. Ask · 02 · Objective in one line

    What it is for, in a single sentence. If it will not fit in one line, it is two requests.

  3. Ask · 03 · Done before?

    If yes, reuse what exists, and stop.

  4. Ask · 04 · Recurring or one-off

    Recurring work is worth a sheet in the library. A one-off needs only a good brief.

  5. Ask · 05 · Shape decided

    What comes back: a table, a draft, a list, a short answer, and how long.

  6. Ask · 06 · Data rule checked

    What must never go in, settled before any material is pasted anywhere.

  7. Build · 07 · Brief filled

    Every blank field becomes a question back to the person asking.

  8. Build · 08 · Prompt assembled

    The brief, set into the structure: role and task, steps, output, stop.

  9. Build · 09 · First run

    Run once, on a normal case.

  10. Build · 10 · Output read for faults

    What is missing, what is wrong, and what it should not have said.

  11. Prove · 11 · Draft gate

    One normal case plus the decline case. Good enough for today's use, and marked as a draft.

  12. Prove · 12 · Library gate

    Five cases, four pass, and the decline holds. The owner, version, limits and what it was tested on are recorded. Then it enters the library.

  13. Prove · 13 · A failure routes back

    Back to the field that failed: the wording, missing context, a new use case, a platform limit or a policy question. The last two are escalated to whoever decides them.

How to tell a rollout is working

A rollout is putting a tool in front of a group of people and helping them use it. Each stage of a rollout answers one question, and each question has measures read from a named source. A measure that cannot be read from a source does not go on the page.

FRAME-04 · Four stages, one question each
FoundationStage 1 ExecutionStage 2 RefinementStage 3 ScaleStage 4
The question log

One row per question asked: who asked, what it really was, how hard it was, and what was done.

The usage dashboards

The figures the tools themselves report.

The library's records

Owner, version, limits and test result for each tested prompt.

The run log

What each test run cost and what it matched.

  1. Foundation

    Which uses matter most, and where do people get stuck?

    • Uses logged and ranked by how often they come up.
    • Prompts tested against an answer key written first.
    • Requests matched to a tested prompt that already exists.
    • A usage baseline by team: where usage stood at the start.

    Read from the question log, the usage dashboards and the library's records.

  2. Execution

    Does it hold up in everyday use?

    • First use within two weeks of a training session where it was shown.
    • Repeat use within 30 days.
    • Recurring questions covered by a tested prompt.
    • Tested prompts passing their cases.

    Read from the usage dashboards, the question log and the run log.

  3. Refinement

    What earns its place, and at what cost?

    • Tested prompts that pass on a smaller model.
    • Cost per use.
    • Prompts retired or merged.

    Read from the run log and the library's records.

  4. Scale

    Where has it changed the work?

    • Impact in people's own words, by team.
    • Uses taken up beyond the first team.
    • Share of people in the air against the baseline.

    Read from the question log and the usage dashboards.

Take any of the four, change the words to fit your work, and keep the structure.
