Ideas worth keeping

A growing library of ideas I have taken from other people: authors, builders and a few good stories. Each card gives what the idea says, where it comes from, and one way to try it, so it works for your life as much as mine.

One spine for each idea on this page. The empty slot at the end is for the next one.

When life changes

Antifragile

From Nassim Nicholas Taleb, Antifragile

Some things break under stress, some survive it, and some grow stronger because of it: muscles, immune systems, a team after a hard quarter. Taleb calls that third kind antifragile.

Try itAfter a setback, ask what it just made stronger, and lean on that.

Who moved my cheese

From Spencer Johnson, Who Moved My Cheese?

In a maze, the cheese keeps moving. Two mice notice early and go looking; one of the little people waits for it to come back. Change comes either way, and the sooner you move with it, the less it costs.

Try itWhen something you rely on changes, picture what its new version looks like, and start looking today.

Peaks and valleys

From Spencer Johnson, Peaks and Valleys

Good times and hard times are connected. Between two peaks there is always a valley, and how you handle the valley decides how soon the next peak comes. Careless choices on a peak often build the next valley.

Try itIn a hard stretch, try seeing it differently, or try the opposite of what led you there. In a good one, keep the habits that got you up.

Finding your direction

Ikigai

From Héctor García and Francesc Miralles, Ikigai

The Japanese word for a reason to get up in the morning. The book draws on Okinawa, where many people live past a hundred and keep a purpose, a circle of friends and daily movement well into old age.

Try itWrite down what made you lose track of time this month. Your ikigai is usually hiding in that list.

Unfair advantage

From Ash Ali and Hasan Kubba, The Unfair Advantage

Nobody starts from the same place. Ali and Kubba group the head starts into five, MILES: money, intelligence and insight, location and luck, education and expertise, and status. Everyone holds some of them; success comes from knowing yours and using them.

Try itScore yourself on each of the five, then build on your strongest one first.

The gap and the gain

From Dan Sullivan and Benjamin Hardy, The Gap and The Gain

Measured against an ideal, you always fall short: that is the gap. Measured against where you started, you see how far you came: that is the gain. Ambitious people tend to live in the gap, and it drains them.

Try itAt the end of a hard day, write down three gains, measured against where you were a year ago.

The makings of greatness

From Jim Hawkins, Treasure Planet

Jim starts as a kid few people expect much from. Long John Silver tells him he has “the makings of greatness”, if he takes the helm and charts his own course. Greatness comes from what you do with the conditions you are handed.

Try itName one condition you did not choose, then pick one course you can steer anyway.

How to think and learn

Under the hood

From Naval Ravikant, The Almanack of Naval Ravikant, compiled by Eric Jorgenson

Know how the engine works before you trust the car. Naval puts foundations above advanced material: understand the basics well enough to rebuild an idea from them, and explain it simply enough for a child to follow.

Try itPick a tool you use every day and learn how one part of it works underneath.

Thinking in systems

From Donella H. Meadows, Thinking in Systems

Most outcomes come from a structure: stocks that build up, flows that fill and drain them, and feedback loops that speed them up or hold them steady. Change the structure and the behaviour changes with it.

Try itFor a problem that keeps coming back, draw what fills it, what drains it, and what loops back.

Grill me, PR and retro

From Matt Pocock, his skills for working with AI

Three habits for working with AI. Grill me: before building, the AI questions you until every decision is made. PR: the change is written up so a reviewer sees the risk first, then the evidence. Retro: after the work, review the session and turn repeated mistakes into checks.

Try itBefore your next big decision, ask someone, or an AI, to question you until nothing is left undecided.

Study outside your field

From Hayao Miyazaki, in a conversation retold on Unpacking Japan

Miyazaki once said the trouble with young animators is that the only thing they study is anime. His generation read Dostoevsky, studied French literature and Impressionism, took it all in, and then made original work. The Shinkansen tells the same story: engineer Eiji Nakatsu reshaped the 500 Series nose after a kingfisher’s beak, and the loud boom of compressed air it made when bursting out of tunnels went away.

Try itSpend an hour this week on something outside your field, then ask what it would change in your own work.

Everyday practice

The two-minute rule

From James Clear, Atomic Habits

A new habit should take less than two minutes to start. “Read every night” becomes “read one page”. Showing up comes first; getting better follows once the habit exists.

Try itShrink the habit you keep putting off until it fits inside two minutes, and do that version today.

Practice makes improvement

From Les Brown, in his talks

Perfection is a state you never reach. What practice gives you is improvement: you can always better your best and go beyond anything you have done before.

Try itCompare this week’s attempt with last month’s, and count the gap as progress.

Ichigo ichie

From Héctor García and Francesc Miralles, The Book of Ichigo Ichie

A saying from the Japanese tea ceremony: one time, one meeting. This moment, with these people, will never happen in quite the same way again, so it deserves your full attention.

Try itIn your next conversation, put the phone away and treat it as the only time you will have it.

Ideas come away from the desk

From Yoshiro Nakamatsu and Marc Benioff

Dr. NakaMats, the Japanese inventor, did his deepest thinking in water and wrote ideas on a waterproof pad. Marc Benioff came up with Salesforce on a six-month sabbatical in India and Hawaii. A change of place gives the mind room.

Try itWhen you are stuck, move: swim, walk, or work somewhere new, and keep something to write on.
